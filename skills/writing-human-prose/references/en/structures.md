## Binary Contrasts

A binary contrast knocks down a claim nobody made, so that the real point arrives as a reversal. The structure supplies the drama the content lacks.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "It's not just X; it's Y." / "not just X but also Y" | Escalation by reflex, since X was never in doubt |
| "Not X. But Y." / "isn't X, it's Y" / "It's not this. It's that." | The contrast used as a sentence template |
| "Not because X. Because Y." / "Not because X, but because Y." | Corrects a cause before anyone proposed the wrong one |
| "[X] isn't the problem. [Y] is." / "The answer isn't X. It's Y." / "The question isn't X. It's Y." | Sets up a target only to knock it over |
| "isn't about X; it's about Y" / "is about X but not Y" | The same pivot routed through "about" |
| "It feels like X. It's actually Y." / "doesn't mean X, but actually Y" / "This doesn't mean X. It means Y." | A reveal staged against an impression the reader never had |
| "stops being X and starts being Y" | A transformation with no mechanism behind it |
| "..., no guessing" / "..., no setup required" (clipped negative tail) | Denies something no one claimed, to make the positive half sound larger |

**Instead:** State Y directly, in a full sentence. If the contrast with X carries real information, fold it in as a clause: "The problem is Y, not X." The test: does the negative half correct a belief the reader actually holds, or one nobody claimed?

## See-Saw Constructions

Slogan templates that balance two halves instead of saying the thing.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "Less X, more Y." | Bumper sticker |
| "More X than Y" (as a reveal) | Same balance trick |
| "Think X, not Y." | Command disguised as insight |
| "It's less about X and more about Y." | Hedged pivot |
| "Work smarter, not harder." (and kin) | Pure cliché |

**Instead:** Write the full sentence with a subject and a verb: "Spend the meeting deciding, and move the status updates to a document."

## Negative Listing

The text lists what something is _not_ before saying what it _is_, so that the answer lands as a reveal.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "Not a X. Not a Y. A Z." | Suspense built from denials the reader didn't need |
| "It wasn't X. It wasn't Y. It was Z." | The same countdown in the past tense |

**Instead:** Say what Z is and skip the denials.

## Dramatic Fragmentation

Fragments cut loose for emphasis promise a weight the content doesn't carry.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "No X. No Y. Just Z." | Reads like ad copy, where fragments sell |
| "[Noun]. That's it. That's the [thing]." | Simplicity performed rather than shown |
| "X. And Y. And Z." | Gives each item a drumroll it hasn't earned |
| "This unlocks something. [Word]." | A reveal with nothing behind it |
| "One word: [word]." | Fake punchline |
| A one-sentence paragraph restating the paragraph before it | Asks for a pause instead of adding a fact |
| The same closer after several sections ("That's the real win.") | Refrain, not argument |

**Instead:** Write full sentences and let the facts do the work. One short sentence carries emphasis when it carries a new fact; cut the one that repeats.

## Over-Compression

The inverse failure. Stripping every sentence to a slogan produces a different kind of slop: the punchy LinkedIn voice. "Move faster. Your competition is." reads as machine-made as the bloat it replaced.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| Every sentence under ten words | Telegraphic monotone |
| Paragraph after paragraph of two-sentence punches | Slide deck, not prose |
| Verbless fragments as standalone sentences | Drama without content |

**Instead:** Let most sentences run 12 to 25 words and carry one idea each, connected by ordinary syntax ("because," "which," "so," "although") where the source gives the link, and by a plain "and" or a period where it gives none. Save the short sentence for the one moment that earns it.

A text corrected for fragments slides easily into the opposite habit: every sentence welded to the next by the same conjunction, "because" at every seam or "which" on every clause, and no sentence left standing on its own. That monotony is as mechanical as the staccato it replaced. Alternate the connective, let a short sentence follow a long one, and never join two facts by a cause or a contrast the source doesn't give: a "but" between a Thursday deploy and a postponed migration invents an opposition, as a "so" invents a consequence.

## The Over-Corrected Register

Over-compression is one move in a larger tell: the "trying not to sound like AI" voice. Replacing the smooth default with a forced-casual default is still a default, and readers clock it as fast as the gloss it replaced.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| A sentence contorted to dodge an em dash, or a colon or ellipsis dropped where the dash was | The bending is as legible as the dash; the fix for a dash is a comma, a period, or parentheses |
| Bolted-on casualness ("lol," a lowercase "i," a swear for flavor) on otherwise-careful prose | Costume, not voice |
| Deliberately-inserted typos ("excyted," "annownce") to beat a detector | Itself a tell, and a worse one |
| Conspicuous lowercase or slang grafted onto a formal piece | The strain reads |

**Instead:** Apply the fixes only as far as a real sentence wants them, and use the comma and period you would actually write. A formal register chosen on purpose is not slop; the tell is the unchosen default, in either direction.

## Paragraph Template

A model builds every paragraph on one mold (topic sentence, explanation, example, transition to the next) and at one length, so the text marches in step even when each sentence passes on its own.

| Pattern | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| Every paragraph opens the same way, by announcing its topic | Open one on a fact, another on a consequence, a third on a reservation |
| Every paragraph runs three or four sentences | Let one paragraph stop at two sentences and another run to seven |
| Every paragraph closes on a transition to the next | Cut the transition where the next paragraph follows on its own; a paragraph can just stop |
| Every section holds the same number of paragraphs | Give each section the length its point needs |

**Instead:** Let the argument set the shape. A paragraph that hands off to the next is ordinary prose; the tell is the handoff served every time, including where the sequence is obvious.

## Rhetorical Question Transitions

The text asks a question only to answer it in the next breath, a transition dressed up as suspense.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "The result? Faster builds." | Fake suspense |
| "The catch? It only works locally." | Same |
| "The best part? It's free." | Ad copy |
| "Why does this matter? Because..." | Self-interview |
| "So what's the answer? Simple." | Double offense |
| "The best part: it learns." | The colon reveal, same move with the question removed |
| "The detail that makes it work: a separate agent grades it." | Noun phrase, colon, dramatic payoff |

**Instead:** State it: "Builds got faster." A question that the next several paragraphs explore can stay; the tell is question-then-instant-answer. The colon reveal gets the same fix, a plain sentence ("A separate agent grades the output, which is what makes it work"); keep colons for lists, labels, and quotes.

## Rhetorical Setups

A setup promises an insight and makes the reader wait for it.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "Sound familiar?" | Assumes a shared experience to win the reader over |
| "What if [reframe]?" / "What if I told you..." | A plain claim staged as a revelation |
| "Think about it:" | Tells the reader to think instead of giving them something to think about |

**Instead:** Make the point and leave the conclusion to the reader. The stock openers that do the same job in one phrase ("Here's what I mean:", "And that's okay.") sit in [phrases.md](phrases.md) under throat-clearing and meta-commentary.

## Arguing with No One

The text answers an objection or rejects an option that appears nowhere else, usually a leftover from an earlier draft or a hedge against an imagined critic.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "I'm not saying X" / "This is not to say" / "This isn't (mainly) about" | Denies a claim no one made |
| "To be clear," / "Don't get me wrong," | Braces for a misreading the text never invited |
| "Some might say... but" / "You might think... but" | Straw objection, then the point |
| "A tempting approach would be" / "One might be tempted to" / "It would be easy to just" | Rejects an option no reader was weighing |

**Instead:** Cut the defense and state the claim it was guarding. Keep an objection the text attributes to someone or answers in full, and keep an alternative a reader would actually weigh. Several unrelated rejections in a row are a stronger sign than one. When in doubt, ask what the sentence answers: if you can name an earlier draft, turn, or edit it replies to, rather than information it gives the reader, cut it.

## Learned Litotes

Affirming by double negation. The construction sounds measured and weakens what it asserts; models use it as a hedge with a scholarly accent.

| Pattern | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| "not without merit" / "not without its challenges" | "useful" / "has challenges," then name them |
| "it is not uncommon for X to" | "X often" |
| "cannot be ignored" / "cannot be overlooked" | "matters," and say why |
| "it is not unreasonable to" | "you can," or state the position |
| "not unlike" | "like" |
| "no small feat" / "no small part" | "hard" / "much of" |

**Instead:** Affirm. One litotes for an ironic understatement is a writer's choice; the tell is the reflex that never states a thing outright.

## Aphorism Templates

An ordinary point dressed as a hidden truth. The shape is borrowed from proverbs; the content is a plain claim in costume.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "X is the language of Y" / "the currency of" / "the architecture of" | Metaphor of abstraction; says nothing about X |
| "X is the Y of Z" ("the Uber of dentistry") | Comparison in place of a description |
| "X becomes a trap" / "X is not a tool but a mirror" | Fake reversal with no mechanism |
| "the deeper issue" / "the heart of the matter" / "the real question" | Announces depth instead of showing it |

**Instead:** Replace the saying with the specific claim: "Users find symmetric layouts easier to predict" beats "Symmetry is the language of trust." The pull-quote check in rules.md catches the same reflex at the sentence level.

## Pseudo-Clefts and Wh- Crutches

The cleft delays the point behind a "what" clause.

| Pattern | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| "What makes this hard is..." | "The constraint is..." or name the specific constraint |
| "What's interesting is that..." | State the interesting thing |
| "What this means is..." | "This means..." or just say it |
| A paragraph opened on "So" by reflex | Open on the content |

A genuine question or a Wh- sentence is fine in moderation; the tell is the cleft used as a reflex to launch sentences.

## Participial Consequence Tails

A comma plus an "-ing" clause hangs a consequence or a judgment on the end of the sentence, with no actor and no evidence: "..., ensuring optimal performance," "..., making it an ideal choice for growing teams." Models attach one to every other sentence; each tail asserts a payoff the sentence never showed.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| ", ensuring [outcome]" | A guarantee nobody made |
| ", making it [judgment]" | Verdict smuggled in as grammar |
| ", highlighting / underscoring / showcasing [significance]" | Significance announced, not shown |
| ", allowing / enabling [someone] to [benefit]" | Benefit claimed without the mechanism |
| ", resulting in [outcome]" (as a reflex) | Causation asserted, not argued |

**Instead:** End the sentence at the fact ("The platform caches every query result") and cut the tail. When the source states a real consequence, give it a clause or a sentence of its own, with an actor. One earned participial clause is ordinary English; the tell is the tail as a sentence-ending reflex.

## Formulaic Constructions

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "By the time X, I was Y." | A stock narrative opener standing in for the story |
| "X that isn't Y" | Describes by what's missing; say what it is ("X is broken") |
| "X: Why Y Matters" (titles) | Colon-headline template |
| "The Power of X" / "Unlocking X" / "A Deep Dive into X" (titles) | Content-farm headline |
| "7 ways to X" / "5 signs that Y" / "N things..." (listicle headlines) | Content-farm scaffolding; write the piece, not the countdown |
| Fixed intro / three body paragraphs / "in summary" conclusion | A template, not a structure; let the order follow the argument, and most pieces need no recap |
| A heading restated by its first sentence ("## Performance," then "Speed matters.") | Runway before the content; cut the restatement |
| A heading over a one- or two-sentence section | Structure heavier than its content; merge the section into a neighbor or drop the heading |
| A top-level heading that repeats the document title | The title stated once is enough |
| Documentation describing what the current text replaced ("added to replace the old loop") | The reader needs the current behavior; history belongs in changelogs and migration guides |

## False Agency

The sentence hands a human action to a thing, so that the grammar hides whoever did the work.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "the data tells us" | Someone read the data and drew the conclusion |
| "the decision emerges" | A person or a group decided |
| "a complaint becomes a fix" | Someone fixed what the customer reported |
| "the market rewards" | Buyers chose to pay |
| "a bet lives or dies in days" | Someone ships the project or drops it |
| "the culture shifts" | People started to behave differently |
| "the conversation moves toward" | Someone steered it |

**Instead:** Give the verb back to the person the text already names: once a paragraph has introduced the support team, "support fixed it" replaces "the complaint becomes a fix." When the text addresses a reader and no one else fits, "you" puts the reader in the seat; when it names no one, keep the impersonal form rather than invent an actor.

The same dodge hides under a thing given a mind. The sentence reads as active and passes a passive-voice check, yet it still names nobody, and it flatters the product on the way, since a dashboard that "understands" does something no dashboard does.

| Pattern | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| "The dashboard understands what your team needs" | Say what the dashboard shows, as far as the text states it |
| "The roadmap wants to focus on retention" | Name who sets the roadmap if the text does; otherwise "Retention is next on the roadmap" |
| "The platform is committed to protecting your data" | Name the company that commits, if the text names one |
| "This approach seeks to reconcile speed and safety" | "This approach reconciles speed and safety," or say how |

The tell starts at a verb that needs a mind behind it: understands, knows, decides, wants, believes, cares, is committed to, aims to. Ordinary product verbs are plain English and stay: "the report shows," "the form submits," "the filter narrows the list" describe what the thing does.

## Passive Voice

Prefer a subject doing something. The tell is passive used to dodge naming a known actor; passive stays legitimate when the actor is unknown, obvious, or beside the point ("he was arrested in May").

| Pattern | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| "It is believed that" | Attribute the belief to whoever holds it |
| "Mistakes were made" | Say who made them |
| "The decision was reached" | Say who decided |
| "X was created" | Say who built it, if the text knows |

**Instead:** Move the actor into the subject slot. The actor must be the one the context designates (the sender of the message, the team named earlier); when the text designates no one, keep the passive rather than invent a subject.

## Narrator-from-a-Distance

The writer comments on events from above instead of staying with the case at hand.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| "People tend to..." | Generalizes about people the text never shows |
| "This is why..." / "This happens because..." (as a reflex) | Turns the text into a lecture addressed to the room |
| "Nobody designed this." | An observation with no observer |

**Instead:** Tie the claim to the case the text is about, with the people and the moment it names. A text that holds no such case keeps the general statement, made once and plainly; never invent a scene to replace it.

## Typographic Emphasis

Decoration doing the work the sentence should do.

| Pattern | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| Bold words scattered through prose | If a word must stand out, rewrite the sentence so it does |
| Bold-label lead-ins (`**Speed:** the build...`) starting list items or paragraphs | Drop the boldface label; write a normal sentence or a real heading |
| Emoji in prose (🚀, ✅, 💡) | Delete; the word carries the meaning |
| Emoji or arrows (→) as bullet markers or section headings | Plain markers and text headings; an emoji inside a sentence is a separate call |
| Em dashes | Comma, period, or parentheses |
| Horizontal-rule dividers (`---`) between sections | Use paragraph breaks or a heading |
| ALL CAPS for emphasis | Rewrite |
| Scare "quotes" around ordinary words | Remove, or commit to the word |

One accent keeps its place: a single all-caps word in a voice that shouts, or one scare quote with a reason behind it. The tell is caps or quotes doing, sentence after sentence, the work the words should do.

## Markup in a Channel That Won't Render It

A plain-text email, a text message, a chat reply, or a form field shows asterisks, hashes, and list dashes as typed. Markup there is a machine signature, since a person writing in those channels doesn't open a paragraph with a hash or bold a word mid-sentence.

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| A heading in hashes opening a block | The sentence that says what the block covers |
| A bold label followed by a colon | The full sentence, or a line on its own with no markup |
| A list in asterisks or dashes | The items inside the sentence, or a plain numbered list if the channel shows numbers |

Check the channel before setting a heading, a bold word, or a bullet. Where the channel renders markdown (documentation, a README, an issue tracker), the usual formatting rules apply. On a social post, the same reflex shows as a "Thread:" or 🧵 opener and a stack of hashtags; cut the opener, and keep at most one or two hashtags inside a sentence.

## Punctuation Habits

| Pattern | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| Semicolons splicing sentences that wanted a period | Period. Reserve the semicolon for two balanced, related clauses |
| Colon as pure announcement ("Here's the key insight:") | Cut the announcement; state the insight |
| Ellipses for trailing drama... | End the sentence |
| Exclamation marks of enthusiasm ("Exciting news!", "Let's go!") | A period; keep the exclamation for a sentence that exclaims |
| Title Case On Common Nouns mid-sentence ("our Marketing Team") | Lowercase; reserve capitals for proper nouns |
| Missing serial comma ("A, B and C") | US convention: "A, B, and C" |
| Hyphen kept on a compound after its noun ("the report is high-quality," "runs in real-time") | Hyphenate before the noun ("a high-quality report") and drop it after ("the report is high quality") |

## Bullet-Point Abuse

A bullet list strips out the connectors ("because," "so," "although") that carry the actual logic.

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| An argument chopped into bullets | The reasoning between the points disappears |
| Two- or three-item lists of related ideas | A connected paragraph says it better |
| Bullets of full paragraphs | Neither a list nor prose; pick one |
| Nested bullets two levels deep in prose | Structure replacing thought |

**Instead:** Keep bullets for parallel items: numbered steps, parameters, an inventory, options of equal rank. For ideas linked by reasoning, write the paragraph and let the syntax show how they connect.

## Rhythm Patterns

| Pattern | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| Triad cadence everywhere ("clear, concise, and compelling") | Vary item counts; two or one often beats three |
| Adjective triads in particular | Cut to the one adjective that earns its place |
| Every paragraph closes on a punch | Let some paragraphs end on a plain fact |
| Three consecutive sentences of matching length | Break one |
| Every sentence opening with the same subject | Vary the opener |

Paragraph length and shape sit under Paragraph Template, and the same conjunction at every seam under Over-Compression.

## Word Patterns

| Pattern | Problem |
| --- | --- |
| A sweeping "always," "never," "everyone," or "nobody" the text can't back | Overclaims; narrow it to what the text supports. A literal "every" ("every request is logged") states a fact and stays |
| Hyphenated compounds stacked on one noun ("an industry-leading, context-aware, best-in-class platform") | Brochure density; keep the compound that names a real property, and cut the rest or name what they stood for |
| Same noun four times in five sentences | Restructure so the noun comes up less often (a pronoun, a merged sentence); don't rotate synonyms |
| Synonym cycling: one referent under rotating names ("the agent reviews the draft, the assistant scores it, the tool suggests fixes") | Elegant variation reads as three things; keep the clear word and repeat it |
