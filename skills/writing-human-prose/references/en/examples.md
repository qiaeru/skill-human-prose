# Before/After Examples

Each "After" is fluent, natural US English: full sentences that connect, concrete subjects and verbs, no AI tells, and no overcorrection into slogans. Sentences join by the link the source gives or, when it gives none, by plain coordination, and the connectives vary from one example to the next. Each "After" contains only what the "Before" supplies: when the source stays vague, the rewrite stays sober instead of inventing a number, a cause, or an actor, and the gap goes to the author in the delivery note. A note mentions that restraint only where it isn't obvious.

## Example 1: Throat-Clearing + Binary Contrast

**Before:**
> "Here's the thing: onboarding is slow. Not because the forms are long. Because nobody answers questions. Let that sink in."

**After:**
> "Onboarding is slow because nobody answers questions, not because the forms are long."

**Changes:** Removed the opener and the emphasis crutch. The source does name the cause it rules out, so the contrast survives as a clause instead of a staged reveal.

## Example 2: Filler + Permission Ending

**Before:**
> "It turns out most migrations run late. The truth is, nobody reads the old code before estimating. And that's okay."

**After:**
> "Most migrations run late, and nobody reads the old code before estimating."

**Changes:** Cut both throat-clearing phrases and the permission the reader never asked for. The two claims sit side by side as the source left them, without a causal link it never stated.

## Example 3: Business Jargon Stack

**Before:**
> "In today's fast-paced landscape, we need to double down on quality and circle back on the roadmap with clarity. This matters because customers are watching."

**After:**
> "We need to put more into quality and revisit the roadmap, since customers are watching."

**Changes:** Replaced the jargon with the plain verbs it stood for, dropped the empty "with clarity," and joined the one reason the source gives to the claim instead of announcing it in a sentence of its own.

## Example 4: Dramatic Fragmentation

**Before:**
> "Two engineers. Forty tickets. One sprint. That's it. That's the plan."

**After:**
> "The plan gives two engineers forty tickets for one sprint."

**Changes:** One complete sentence carries the three facts, with no performative fragments and no em dash.

## Example 5: Rhetorical Setup

**Before:**
> "What if I told you the fastest code reviews aren't the shortest ones? Here's what I mean: they're the ones with a clear description. Think about it."

**After:**
> "The fastest code reviews are the ones with a clear description, not the shortest ones."

**Changes:** Stated the claim directly and dropped the staged question, the preview, and the prompt. The source gives no reason for the claim, so the rewrite adds none.

## Example 6: AI Vocabulary

**Before:**
> "This robust solution leverages cutting-edge AI to seamlessly streamline your workflow and unlock your team's full potential."

**After:**
> "The tool uses AI to simplify your workflow."

**Changes:** Replaced six AI-signature words with the plain claim underneath them; "unlock your team's full potential" asserted nothing and went. What the tool does and what the team gains stay for the author to name.

## Example 7: Rhetorical Question Transition

**Before:**
> "We rewrote the build pipeline in Rust. The result? Builds that used to take twenty minutes now finish in four. The catch? Nobody on the team knew Rust."

**After:**
> "We rewrote the build pipeline in Rust, and builds that used to take twenty minutes now finish in four, although nobody on the team knew Rust."

**Changes:** Removed both question-answer transitions; ordinary syntax carries the same surprise, and "although" carries the concession "The catch" already made.

## Example 8: See-Saw + Bold Emphasis

**Before:**
> "Less meetings, more shipping. The key insight is that **focus** beats **alignment** every single time."

**After:**
> "Hold fewer meetings and ship more; focus matters more than alignment."

**Changes:** Replaced the bumper-sticker balance, the announced "key insight," the bold emphasis, and the lazy extreme ("every single time") with two plain clauses. The source sets the two claims side by side, so a semicolon joins them where "because" would add a reason.

## Example 9: Negative Listing

**Before:**
> "This isn't a feature. It isn't a product. It's a whole new way of working."

**After:**
> "This is a new way of working."

**Changes:** Dropped the two denials and the intensifier "whole," and stated the claim. The source never says what changes or for whom, so the rewrite stays at that level, and the delivery note asks the author to name it.

## Example 10: Passive Voice Hiding the Actor

**Before:**
> "It was decided that the deadline would be moved, and users will be notified once a new date has been established."

**After:**
> "We moved the deadline and will notify users as soon as we set the new date."

**Changes:** Named the actor once and let it govern both verbs. The actor is the one the context designates, the sender of the message, and the notification channel stays unspecified since the source doesn't name it.

## Example 11: Stacked Hedges

**Before:**
> "This approach could arguably, in many ways, be seen as somewhat more effective, at least to some extent, than the previous one."

**After:**
> "This approach is probably more effective than the previous one."

**Changes:** Five reflexive hedges became one. The source expresses doubt, so a single hedge stays rather than none.

## Example 12: Connector Stacking

**Before:**
> "Moreover, the new API is faster. Furthermore, it requires fewer credentials. Additionally, it is worth noting that the documentation has been improved."

**After:**
> "The new API is faster, asks for fewer credentials, and comes with better documentation."

**Changes:** Deleted all three connector openers and the filler; one sentence carries the list. "Improved" stays as vague as the source left it.

## Example 13: Marketing Opener

**Before:**
> "In a world where attention is the scarcest resource, whether you're a solo founder or a Fortune 500 executive, this newsletter is for you."

**After:**
> "This newsletter is for founders and executives, whatever the size of their company."

**Changes:** Cut the cosmic opener and the "whether you're" balancing act. The audience stays the one the source describes rather than a narrower reader invented for concreteness.

## Example 14: Vague Declarative

**Before:**
> "The implications of this change are significant, and the stakes for the industry are high."

**After:**
> "This change will affect the industry."

**Changes:** Removed both announcements of importance. The source never says what the implications are, so the rewrite stops at the claim it supports, and the delivery note asks the author to name the implication (who has to change what, by when).

## Example 15: Triad Cadence

**Before:**
> "Our documentation is clear, concise, and compelling. The onboarding is fast, friendly, and frictionless. The product is simple, scalable, and secure."

**After:**
> "The documentation is short and clear, onboarding takes little time, and the product stays simple and secure as it scales."

**Changes:** Broke three consecutive alliterative triads into one sentence with uneven items, dropping the adjectives that said nothing ("compelling," "friendly") and keeping the claims that do ("clear," "fast," "secure").

## Example 16: Over-Compression

**Before:**
> "Ship daily. Talk to users. Cut scope. Repeat. That's the job. Everything else is noise."

**After:**
> "The job is to ship every day, talk to users, cut scope, and then start over."

**Changes:** The slogans became one flowing sentence with the same four moves in the same order, and the closing dichotomy goes. The source doesn't say that cutting scope serves the shipping, so the rewrite lists the moves without making one the reason for another.

## Example 17: Cliché Closer

**Before:**
> "In conclusion, remote work is here to stay. Only time will tell how organizations will adapt, but one thing is certain: the future of work has arrived."

**After:**
> "Remote work will last, and organizations are still working out how to adapt to it."

**Changes:** Replaced three closing clichés with the two claims underneath them.

## Example 18: Pull-Quote Bait

**Before:**
> "Culture isn't what you say. It's what you tolerate. And what you tolerate becomes who you are."

**After:**
> "A team's culture shows in what it tolerates rather than in what it says about itself, and over time the tolerated behavior becomes the norm."

**Changes:** The aphorism chain became one working sentence; the insight survived without the poster format and without an example the source doesn't give.

## Example 19: Bulleted Argument

**Before:**
> We should migrate this quarter:
>
> - The old API is deprecated
> - Support ends in June
> - The team has capacity now
> - Waiting increases risk

**After:**
> "We should migrate this quarter: the old API is deprecated and loses support in June, the team has capacity now, and waiting increases the risk."

**Changes:** The list hid the reasoning. The sentence keeps the source's frame, a decision followed by its reasons, joins the two facts about the old API, and leaves each point at the level the source gives it.

## Example 20: Impersonal Directives

**Before:**
> "It is important to note that there are several settings that must be configured before the first run. It is recommended that a backup be performed."

**After:**
> "Configure the required settings before the first run, and back up your data first."

**Changes:** Two impersonal openers and an expletive "there are" became direct instructions. The source doesn't list the settings, so the rewrite leaves the list to the author rather than guess at it.

## Example 21: Bolded Lead-In Labels

**Before:**
> "Our process has three stages. **Discovery:** we interview your users. **Design:** we prototype the top flows. **Delivery:** we ship and measure."

**After:**
> "Our process runs in three stages: we interview your users, prototype the flows that matter most, then ship them and measure the result."

**Changes:** Dropped the bold-label scaffolding that formats a chat answer as prose; the connected sentence carries the same three stages in the same order.

## Example 22: Both-Sides Menu

**Before:**
> "Should you use a monorepo? On one hand, it simplifies dependency management. On the other hand, it complicates CI. Ultimately, it depends on your needs."

**After:**
> "Use a monorepo when simpler dependency management is worth a more complicated CI, and skip it when it isn't."

**Changes:** Replaced the non-committal menu with a stance built on the one trade-off the source names. The source doesn't say where that trade-off tips, so the rewrite states the condition in the source's own terms and leaves the threshold to the author.

## Example 23: Participial Consequence Tails

**Before:**
> "The platform caches every query result, ensuring optimal performance and allowing your team to focus on what matters, making it an ideal solution for growing businesses."

**After:**
> "The platform caches every query result."

**Changes:** The three tails claimed payoffs the sentence never showed ("optimal performance," "focus on what matters," "ideal solution"), so the sentence ends on its fact. A measured gain, if the author has one, can follow in a sentence of its own.

## Example 24: Email Boilerplate

**Before:**
> "I hope this email finds you well. I wanted to reach out to follow up on the proposal I sent last week. Please don't hesitate to reach out if you have any questions. Thank you for your patience!"

**After:**
> "Have you had a chance to look at the proposal I sent last week? If any part of it needs explaining, tell me which one."

**Changes:** Cut the stock opener, the double "reach out," and the canned closers; the email now opens with the ask and ends with a concrete next step.

## Example 25: Arguing with No One

**Before:**
> "I'm not saying code review is a waste of time. Don't get me wrong, some teams get a lot out of it. One might be tempted to drop it entirely, but that would be an overreaction. The point is that a review queue longer than a day slows every merge behind it."

**After:**
> "A review queue longer than a day slows every merge behind it."

**Changes:** Three sentences fended off objections the text never received (that review is useless, that it should be dropped), then a fourth announced the point. The claim they guarded stands on its own.

## Example 26: Borrowed Authority and Inflated Significance

**Before:**
> "Experts agree that the 2019 redesign marked a pivotal moment in the company's history, setting the stage for its later growth. The product has since been featured in TechCrunch, Wired, The Verge, and several trade publications."

**After:**
> "The company redesigned the product in 2019 and has grown since. TechCrunch, Wired, and The Verge have covered it."

**Changes:** The unnamed experts and the two significance phrases went; the redesign, its date, and the later growth are the source's own. The three named outlets stay as facts, and the unnamed "trade publications" go, since the source can't say which.

## Example 27: Knowledge-Gap Guess

**Before:**
> "While details about her early career are not widely documented, she likely began in local journalism before moving to policy work, which appears to have shaped her later focus on housing."

**After:**
> "Her later work focused on housing."

**Changes:** The disclaimer and the two guesses hung on it ("likely," "appears to have") went, leaving the one fact the text states. The missing career history goes to the author in the delivery note, not into the text as a guess or a disclaimer.

## Example 28: Vague Connection and Copula Dodge

**Before:**
> "She is associated with the Northside Food Bank, which she founded in 2015 and continues to run. The organization serves as the main distribution point for the district and boasts over forty volunteers."

**After:**
> "She founded the Northside Food Bank in 2015 and still runs it. It is the district's main distribution point and has more than forty volunteers."

**Changes:** "Associated with" gave way to the relationship the source itself names a few words later; "serves as" and "boasts" became "is" and "has."

## Example 29: Faux-Insight Setups and Colon Reveals

**Before:**
> "Here's what nobody tells you about onboarding: the first week sets the tone. The part most people miss: new hires copy whoever sits next to them. The best part: you can choose who that is."

**After:**
> "The first week of onboarding sets the tone. New hires copy whoever sits next to them, and you can choose who that is."

**Changes:** Three setups announced an insight each time ("what nobody tells you," "the part most people miss," "the best part") and hung each payoff on a colon. The three claims stand on their own as two plain sentences, side by side as the source left them.

## Example 30: Synonym Cycling

**Before:**
> "The agent reads each pull request as it opens. The assistant then leaves a comment on any file over 500 lines. Finally, the bot posts a summary to the channel, and the tool closes stale requests after a week."

**After:**
> "The agent reads each pull request as it opens, comments on any file over 500 lines, and posts a summary to the channel. After a week, it closes stale requests."

**Changes:** One program carried four names in four sentences (agent, assistant, bot, tool), and a reader could take them for four programs. The rewrite keeps the first name and lets a shared subject and a pronoun carry the rest.

## Example 31: Reader Guidance and Fake-Profound Kicker

**Before:**
> "We moved the nightly build to run on merge, and review time dropped from thirty minutes to eight. Engineers stopped batching their changes. That last part matters more than it sounds. In the end, speed is a culture, not a setting."

**After:**
> "We moved the nightly build to run on merge, and review time dropped from thirty minutes to eight. Engineers stopped batching their changes."

**Changes:** The guidance line told the reader how much weight to give the sentence before it, and the closing aphorism dressed two facts as a lesson, with a contrast on the way out. Both go rather than get rewritten into a better line, so the piece ends on its last concrete sentence.

## Example 32: Paragraph Template

**Before:**
> "The migration took three weeks. The team first froze writes, then switched reads, then cut the old database over. The freeze ran forty minutes longer than planned. That explains the gap on Monday.
>
> The training took two days. The team first covered the new schema, then walked through the common queries, then opened a question session. The session ran an hour over. That explains the fatigue on Tuesday."

**After:**
> "The migration took three weeks: the team froze writes, switched reads, then cut the old database over. The freeze ran forty minutes longer than planned, which is where Monday's gap came from.
>
> The training, two days of it, covered the new schema and the common queries before a question session that ran an hour over and left the team tired on Tuesday."

**Changes:** Both paragraphs followed one mold, a duration, three steps on "first, then, then," a timed slip, and a closer on "That explains." The rewrite keeps every fact and figure, gives each paragraph its own shape and length, and drops the repeated closer while keeping what each one said: Monday's gap moves into a relative clause, and Tuesday's fatigue joins the overrun it came from. The source never says the training came after the migration, so the rewrite doesn't either.

## Example 33: Thing Given a Mind and Credentials Opener

**Before:**
> "As someone who has run support teams for a decade, I can tell you the dashboard understands what your team needs. The platform is committed to surfacing the right numbers, and the roadmap wants to focus on retention next."

**After:**
> "The dashboard shows the numbers your team needs. The platform is designed to surface the right ones, and retention is next on the roadmap."

**Changes:** The opener put the writer's standing ahead of a product claim, and three things carried a mind of their own: a dashboard that understands, a platform that commits, a roadmap that wants. The rewrite says what each thing does or is for, in impersonal form, since the source names no company and a writer who ran support teams can't speak for the vendor as "we." Which numbers the team needs stays as vague as the source left it.
